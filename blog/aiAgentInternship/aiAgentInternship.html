Designing for AI Agents 🤖

Designing for AI Agents 🤖 — Taobao Shangou (formerly Ele.me): can you really order a meal by just talking?
Bottom line

This internship at Taobao Shangou (formerly Ele.me) started with one question: can you really order a meal just by talking to a speaker? I took that requirement apart until it named the missing capability and the missing experience, built a minimum viable version on an open-source voice-assistant project, and dug out every point on the journey where an agent could genuinely improve the experience — then handed the team a design-led technical roadmap. The voice agent was the main line, not the whole job: I also designed the AI search entry, generated filter labels with a model, walked through AI coding tools from a designer's point of view, and brought the method back to the team. Each of them holds the same anchor: an AI capability is only worth something if users notice it, trust it enough to use, and it really solves their problem.

  2. Hold the value anchor. The anchor was "can one sentence actually get the meal ordered". When the technology could not do it I did not quietly lower the bar; when the technology looked impressive I did not let that count as progress. Everything went back to the anchor.
  3. Design can lead engineering — if it holds the criteria. Writing the experience requirements down as testable criteria rather than preferences is what gave design a seat in the roadmap. To argue feasibility honestly, I put a quarter of my own time into the build.

00What I Actually Did

Six workstreams, one anchor

Taobao Shangou (formerly Ele.me) was building an Agent for pure-voice commerce: you speak to a Tmall Genie smart speaker and the meal is ordered, with no screen anywhere in the loop. I was on the consumer-facing experience design team from May to September 2025. Voice was the main line — not the only one.

01Taking the Requirement Apart

From a feature request to two engineering shortcuts

"Build a voice-ordering feature" is not a brief, it is a proposed solution. The actual requirement is that one sentence gets the meal ordered. I took that down one level: to do it, the user's side needs three capabilities — understanding intent (including vague phrasings), autonomous execution across several steps, and speaking the result back. Each one leans on different machinery — speech recognition, intent classification, model reasoning, tool calls, retrieval. Once it was laid out that way the problem stopped being vague: two of the three were roughly available, and the one that was not was autonomous execution.

The target chain. The middle — autonomous execution — is the only link the technology was not ready to carry.
Bottleneck 01

Response latency

Average deep-reasoning time on a large model already exceeds what people tolerate while speaking. Silence on a call reads as a dropped line; silence from a speaker reads as broken.

Bottleneck 02

Hallucination

A model given autonomy will sometimes fabricate facts, and that is largely uncontrollable — the exact opposite of what a payment flow needs.

To buy controllability, the platform absorbed both bottlenecks with two engineering shortcuts: a fixed task flow — the flow decides which step runs, not the model — and templated replies at the feedback stage, where the model only fills in the blanks. Reasonable choices. Both also convert a technical limitation into something users hit head-on.

Symptom 01

Vague instructions fail

"Add one more of the other one" has no step that resolves "the other one". The user ends up negotiating with a system that cannot hold the reference.

Symptom 02

Coverage stops at the flow

Anything outside the pre-set steps is not refused — it is simply absent. Users cannot tell "we don't offer that" from "I wasn't built for that".

Symptom 03

The language feels mechanical

Templated replies are correct and lifeless. In a voice-only product phrasing is the entire interface, so mechanical phrasing reads as low intelligence.

The same three failures on the live product. None is a wording problem — each is the visible edge of an architectural decision.
The trade-off I named

The product was buying controllability with the feeling of intelligence — and nobody had chosen the exchange rate. Both are real requirements, but the trade-off had been made by the architecture rather than by a decision. Naming it was the first useful thing I did: a trade-off can be renegotiated, an architecture can only be accepted.

Not "the agent isn't smart enough" — "it is buying controllability with intelligence, and nobody decided how much".

02Holding the Anchor: Build It

A prototype built to locate the ceiling, not to ship

Does a technical bottleneck mean we cannot build a good experience? On the evidence, no: companion products on the market — AI pets, robots — already carry the fluency and error tolerance voice interaction needs, and published benchmarks put Gemini 2.0's hallucination rate at 0.7%. But that is not a claim you settle by arguing. It has to be settled by running it. So I built it: an open-source voice assistant (XiaoZhi AI) standing in for the Tmall Genie ordering flow, wired to a real LLM.

Experience first, technology after: prove the core journey works, then work backwards to the production stack.

Two different problems hid behind one demo. Making it hear and speak was configuration — the Vosk speech model, plus a role prompt that moved the assistant from generic chatting to answering food-ordering questions. Making it execute was retrieval: the model did not need to be taught how to order food, it needed to be told what the platform concretely offers. Three moves carried the whole build — configure a role prompt for the model, stand up a mock database, and write mock tool scripts.

From cannot hear or speak to can hear and speak. Structure, not verbosity, is what changed its behaviour.
Mock nearby-merchant and historical-order data standing in for the knowledge base; mock business scripts exposed so the model could actually search, edit a cart and place an order.
The two main voice-ordering scenarios both moved: search-and-recommend went from no specific product to a real recommendation; reorder went from no memory of past orders to a simulated repeat purchase based on preference.
What the MVP settled

Roughly 80% of the core voice-ordering experience ran end to end, and three key experiences came out verified: the agent can reply in varied natural language, can resolve ambiguous intent, and can execute a fairly complex task autonomously. The gap was real — and it was closable.

03Working Backwards to a Roadmap

The actual deliverable

A demo is not a deliverable; the argument it supports is. Having shown the journey could work, I turned it around and wrote down what the experience demanded of the technology — as criteria, not preferences, because criteria can be tested and preferences can only be argued about. Then I costed each direction into something engineering could start on tomorrow.

Three experience criteria, each mapped to an optimisation direction and a first concrete step. This is the artefact I would point to as the internship's real output.
Direction worked backwards Transitional move engineering can ship now Criterion it serves
Reduce the mechanical feel of templated replies Ship several switchable persona-style reply templates instead of one fixed form. Variety in natural language
Add cross-scenario capability Extend the model to support switching shop and switching category mid-conversation. Tolerance of ambiguous intent · autonomous multi-step execution

The third criterion — autonomous multi-step execution — stayed as the long pole. It is the premise of pure-voice ordering and the one capability the technology was least ready to give, so it was written down as the target rather than promised as a date.

04The Other Lines

Seen, tapped, and actually buildable

Voice was the main line. The rest attacked the same anchor from different sides: an AI capability has to be noticed, has to be worth tapping, and has to be something a designer can reach. Two of them started the same way — a competitor walkthrough and a survey, so the premise was evidence rather than taste.

Seen. The survey found 66% of users had never noticed the AI entry (it only appeared as a floating layer on the results page) and 43% could not tell AI search from normal search. So the entry moved into the suggestion list, and the suggestion words themselves became the next thing to design.
Tapped. Filter labels are generated on two axes — scenario (time of day, local character, environment) × user (preference): a hot-weather scenario for an urban office worker produces cold-brew iced coffee. The experiment is still open — the next step needs the test cohort and the product-matching rule pinned down.
Reachable. Tools compared from a non-developer's point of view plus developer interviews, landing on one working loop: AI explains code to me, I supply specification to the AI — used directly on the MVP, by handing the model a protocol spec and letting it fix the code around it.

05Passing It On

Back to the team

Work that stays inside one project only pays once. So the method and the wider context both went back to the team — a workshop on how the voice-ordering MVP was actually built, and a briefing on the AI-glasses market for the weekly AI news session.

The method, not the demo. The workshop took apart what the prototype needed: three capabilities to attack, and the ways I attacked them.
Wider context. Summarising a weekly AI briefing on the AI-glasses market — the consumer view of where the sector actually is.

06What I Took Away

Reflection
  1. Take requirements apart until they name a capability. "Build a voice feature" is a solution. Breaking it open is what revealed that the missing piece was autonomous execution, not better copy.
  2. Never trade the anchor away quietly. When the technology fell short I did not lower the bar by stealth; when it looked impressive I did not count that as progress.
  3. Hold the criteria and design gets a say in engineering. Testable criteria, each costed into a move engineering can ship now, are what let a design document become a technical roadmap.
  4. AI work still sits on the platform's own rails. Every AI feature I touched was built on the platform's existing core journeys and functional nodes — which is why the conventional work (the in-store payment-success page) mattered: there, the interaction spec still has to be 100% complete.
  5. State what your own prototype cannot do. The MVP covered the core experience only — no real merchant interfaces, payment interfaces or production data — so it could not close the loop. Writing that down is what made it credible.
How the role actually split — three quarters design, one quarter technology — and the collaboration model behind it: design leads and pulls engineering, rather than handing a specification across.
