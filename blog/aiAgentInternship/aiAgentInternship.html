Designing for AI Agents 🤖

Designing for AI Agents 🤖 — Taobao Shangou (formerly Ele.me): from a voice-ordering demo to a technical roadmap
Bottom line

From May to September 2025 I was on the consumer-facing experience design team at Taobao Shangou (formerly Ele.me), owning the voice-interaction workstream of the Tmall Genie smart takeaway-ordering project — ordering a meal by talking to a smart speaker, with no screen anywhere in the loop. The question I was handed was not "design a voice feature" but a harder one: can a pure-voice transaction actually work, and if not, what exactly is missing? The shipped agent suggested not. It was pinned to a fixed task flow with templated replies, so any request outside the script simply failed and the answers read like a form letter — and no rewriting of the copy could fix that, because the limit was architectural rather than linguistic. To locate the real ceiling instead of guessing at it, I built a working MVP on a real large language model that ran the entire ordering loop, then used it to derive — from evidence — which capabilities the technology has to grow next. The internship closed with a design-led technical roadmap: three experience principles, each mapped to an engineering direction and a first action. My own boundary moved with it — roughly three quarters design, one quarter hands-on engineering.

00What I Did and Why

The remit, the finding, and what it turned into

Taobao Shangou (formerly Ele.me) is building an Agent capability for pure-voice commerce: you speak to a Tmall Genie smart speaker and the meal is ordered, with no screen in the loop. The demo was convincing; the live product kept failing in the same two places — requests that did not match a pre-set flow could not be served, and the replies sounded like a form letter. Both symptoms had one root: the agent was not allowed to reason freely, because reasoning freely is slow and occasionally invents things. My remit was to establish whether that ceiling is a design problem or a technical one — the question the whole internship was organised around.

01Experiencing the Constraint

Discipline: problem framing & technical literacy

The target architecture is worth stating plainly, because everything that follows is a response to it. A user speaks to a Tmall Genie smart speaker; the device transcribes the speech; the agent recognises the intent; a large language model reasons about it; and a set of platform tools then executes — searching products, recommending items, editing the cart, confirming the order and taking payment. The user hears the result read back. On paper it is a complete conversational commerce loop.

The intended workflow. The interesting part is the middle: autonomous execution against a set of platform tools. That is the capability the whole experience depends on, and the one the technology was least ready to give.
Limit 01

Response latency

Deep reasoning on a large model takes longer than people tolerate in a spoken conversation. Silence on a phone call feels broken; the same silence from a speaker reads as malfunction.

Limit 02

Hallucination

A model given autonomy will sometimes invent. The shorter the response time, the more it invents — so the two limits pull against each other rather than trading off cleanly.

The two structural limits, and why both land directly on the product rather than staying abstract: latency is experienced as brokenness, and hallucination is experienced as being lied to.

02Where the Experience Broke

Discipline: usability diagnosis

In production those limits had been absorbed by two coping strategies — a fixed task flow and templated replies. Both are reasonable engineering responses. Both also convert a technical limitation into a user-visible failure, and the failures were specific enough to name.

Symptom 01

Ambiguous instructions fail

"Add one more of the other one" cannot be carried out by a fixed flow, because the flow has no step that resolves "the other one". The user is left negotiating with a system that cannot hold the reference.

Symptom 02

Coverage stops at the flow

Anything outside the pre-set steps is simply unavailable — not refused, just absent. Users cannot tell the difference between "we don't do that" and "I wasn't built for that".

Symptom 03

The language feels mechanical

Templated replies are correct and lifeless. In a voice-only product, phrasing is the entire interface, so mechanical phrasing reads as low intelligence.

The same three failures as they appeared on the live product, illustrated with voice-assistant transcripts. None of them is a wording problem — each is the visible edge of a structural decision.
The tension I named

The product was optimising for controllability at the direct expense of the feeling of intelligence. Both are real requirements — an agent that acts on a misheard order is worse than one that asks again. But the trade-off had been made implicitly, by the architecture, rather than chosen. Naming it as a trade-off was the first useful thing I did, because a trade-off can be moved and an architecture can be argued with.

The framing that unlocked the work: not "the agent is not smart enough" but "the agent is buying controllability with intelligence, and nobody decided how much".

03Breaking the Deadlock

Discipline: prototyping & systems configuration
The question I put to myself

Does a current technical limit mean we cannot build a good product experience? I did not think the answer was yes — and more importantly, I did not think it could be settled by argument. It had to be settled by building the thing and seeing where it actually broke.

The approach: build the MVP on an open-source voice-assistant project standing in for the Tmall Genie ordering flow, run the core experience first, and only then reverse-engineer the route to a real production stack. Experience proves the case; technology follows it.

The tool choice was deliberate. I needed something genuinely voice-interactive — a project that could already hear and speak, so the conversation was the medium rather than a simulation of one — and I needed it to be open source, so the parts that mattered could be reconfigured rather than merely used. Everything past that point was a design decision about which capabilities the experience could not do without.

Separating the agent's three core capabilities — intent recognition, autonomous execution, result feedback — from the machinery that implements them. That separation is what made the build tractable: only one of the three is really the model's job.
Making it hear and speak. A wake-word and speech-recognition configuration took the MVP from cannot hear or speak to can hear and speak; a Markdown-structured prompt with an explicit skill scope moved it from generic chatting to answering ordering questions. Structure, not verbosity, was what changed its behaviour.

Making it execute was a different problem from making it talk. Here the useful frame was a quadrant: does the model know the business domain or not, and does it know the specific facts or not? Prompt engineering covers what the model already understands; where it does not, the answer is not more prompting but retrieval-augmented generation — giving it material instead of instructions.

The reasoning that chose the technique rather than defaulting to one: the agent did not need better instructions about how to order, it needed to be told what the platform concretely offers — which is a retrieval problem, not a prompting one.
Implementing the retrieval layer on both sides: mock merchant and order data standing in for a real knowledge base (shop, time, location, specification), and tool scripts exposed to the model so it could actually search, edit a cart and place an order. The scripts were written with AI assistance — the same skill the internship was meant to build.
What the MVP proved. Two cases that the fixed flow could not handle — ordering something nearby, and reordering something before — became specific product recommendations and a working repeat-purchase flow. The capability gap was real, and it was closable.

04Turning the Demo into a Roadmap

Discipline: experience criteria → technical plan

A demo is not a deliverable on its own; its value is the argument it supports. Having shown that the core journey could work, I turned it around and asked what the experience demanded of the technology — then wrote those demands down as criteria rather than preferences, so that engineering could be held to them and design could be accountable to them.

The reverse-engineered roadmap. Three experience criteria — natural language variety, recognition of ambiguous intent, and autonomous execution of more complex tasks — each mapped to an optimisation direction and a first concrete step. This is the artefact I would point to as the internship's real output: a design document that engineering could act on.

05Redefining the Design Role

Discipline: collaboration model & professional boundary

The most transferable thing I learned was not about agents. It was about what happens to the design function when the product's core difficulty is technical. In the conventional model, design sits between product and engineering and hands over a specification. In this work, design had to pull engineering rather than hand off to it, because the question on the table — what should the experience demand of the model — could only be answered by someone holding both the experience intent and enough technical literacy to argue about feasibility.

Shift 01

From answering to exploring

Moving from passively responding to a specification toward proactively opening the question. The brief was not "design this flow" — it was "this flow cannot work; find out why".

Shift 02

Technology must prove product value

Treating technical work as instrumental: a capability earns its place by what it makes possible for the user, not by being impressive. That is the same test a design decision has to pass.

The role as it actually split — three quarters design, one quarter technology — and the collaboration model that came out of it, with the limitations written down honestly: the MVP covers core interaction logic only and does not connect to real merchant systems, payment or data, so it cannot close the loop in production. Stating that limit was part of the work.

06Practising What I Was Preaching

Discipline: AI-assisted practice

Arguing that designers should work with AI only means something if you do it on your own work. I ran a small study comparing two AI coding tools from a non-developer's point of view — reading the code, setting up a project, editing, running it — and interviewed three professional developers to check where my reading of the tools was naive. The useful finding was a working loop rather than a verdict: AI explains code so I can learn it, and I supply the specifications so the AI can learn them.

From comparison to practice. The two-way loop — AI explains code to me, I supply specification to the AI — is what let me contribute on the technical side without pretending to be a developer. I used it directly in the voice project, for instance to work out an interface script whose protocol format I did not know, by showing the model a comparable specification.

The same instinct applied to a second workstream. Product filters were too coarse to mean anything to an individual user, so rather than invent better labels by taste I built a generation pipeline: define what dimensions to collect, assemble a user portrait, define the model's role in a prompt, then take structured output as candidate filters.

The generation pipeline, with the purpose annotated at every step rather than only the method. Collecting scenario and preference dimensions gives the model evidence; the user portrait makes output relevant; the role prompt makes it well-formed; and the final step turns plausible language into shippable filter labels.
Why personalisation, specifically

The design rationale was not "personalisation is good". It was the observation that self-relevance is what makes a filter label attractive — a category that names your situation earns a tap in a way a category that names a food type does not. That is a testable claim about attention, which is why the work ended with a pilot rather than a conclusion.

The same lesson applied to an entry point: an AI capability that users never notice is worth nothing, so the demo moved the AI search entry earlier in the journey, into the suggestion list, where it can be seen before the user has committed to a query.

07What I Took Away

Reflection
  1. Constraints are design material, not obstacles. The agent's limitations were the most interesting thing about it, and treating them as a brief — rather than a complaint — is what produced the only substantial output of the internship.
  2. Build the question before answering it. The MVP did not exist to be shipped. It existed so the argument about the technology would be grounded in something demonstrable.
  3. Design can lead technical work when it holds the criteria. Writing the experience requirements down as criteria — not preferences — is what gave design a say in the roadmap.
  4. State the limits of your own prototype. Recording what the MVP could not do (no real merchant systems, no payment, no data layer) was what made it credible rather than oversold.
  5. AI is a working method, not a topic. The most useful AI work was using it on my own process — comparing tools, learning from generated code, and building generation pipelines — rather than only designing AI features.
