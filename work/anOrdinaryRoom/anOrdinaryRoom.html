AN ORDINARY ROOM 🚪 - MR Simulated Scene with Interactive Projection Mapping
Bottom line

Stop describing the fear and build the room where it happens. AN ORDINARY ROOM reconstructs a domestic scene in mixed reality — a real couch, a real wall, a window with daylight — and then projects a single cockroach onto it, testing whether a projection can produce a bodily reaction that an explanation never could.

00Executive Summary

What this piece is, and why it works

This is an ordinary room. We sit on the couch. We talk. We laugh. We notice something approaching. We are scared and we scream. We finally slap. This is an ordinary, normal life. And then, in the middle of an ordinary conversation, a cockroach scurries into the room.

The fear it elicits is universal and transcends cultural boundaries. The project's goal is to recreate that experience virtually — triggering memories for those who have met these resilient creatures, and introducing the sensation to those who have not.

  1. Fear is the material, not the subject. The piece never argues that people are afraid of cockroaches. It engineers the conditions for the reaction to happen in the room, to the person in it. The claim is made through the body rather than through text.
  2. The mundane is the control condition. A white-covered couch in a darkened corner is deliberately unremarkable. The whole mechanism is the gap between an ordinary setting and an intruding creature — remove the domesticity and the effect collapses.
  3. The mapping leaves the furniture. Calibrated onto a soft, non-planar couch and the wall behind it, the projection also falls across whoever is sitting there. Digital layer and visitor share one surface, so the boundary between the simulated room and the real one stops being legible.
  4. Behaviour, not decoration. The cockroach is animated as an agent — approaching, scuttling, retreating — so the viewer's anxiety has something to track. What has to be convincing is the movement, not the rendering.

01Design Perspective

Discipline: design & narrative

The design problem is not “how do we show a cockroach” — that is a drawing task. It is how do we make someone flinch in a gallery, using nothing but light on furniture. Every decision below follows from taking that seriously.

Design decision What it is Why it was made
Start from a universal trigger The cockroach, chosen as a fear that crosses cultures. No cultural translation is needed, so the piece can work directly on memory instead of spending time on explanation.
Build the ordinary first A plain room, a white-covered couch, daylight through a window. The mundane is the baseline. An intrusion only reads as an intrusion against something calm — so the set had to be believable before anything was projected.
Trigger memory rather than teach Two audiences are addressed at once. Those who have met the creature get a remembered reaction; those who have not get the sensation for the first time. One mechanism, two experiences.
Aim at reflection, not a jump scare The encounter invites the visitor to notice their own response. The piece asks the viewer to consider their visceral reaction — fear, curiosity, perhaps even respect for the creature's survival instincts. Self-observation is the ending.
Design intent

We invite you to step into a digitalised ordinary space, where the unexpected meets the mundane. The projected room is not a horror set and not a nature documentary — it is a reconstruction of a corner everyone already recognises, with one thing wrong in it.

02Technical Perspective

Discipline: mixed reality & projection mapping

Technically the work is a mixed-reality setup rather than a VR piece: the room, the couch and the light through the window are physically present, and only the creature is digital. Keeping the furniture real means the visitor's body is already convinced before the first frame of animation appears.

Component 01

Mixed-reality staging

A real couch, wall and window sit inside a darkened room. Only one element of the scene is simulated, which keeps the visitor's expectations anchored in the physical space they can touch.

Component 02

Interactive projection mapping

The animation is calibrated to a soft, non-planar surface — a fabric-covered couch that flexes when someone sits — plus the wall and floor behind it. Mapping onto a moving, curved surface is the core technical difficulty of the piece.

Component 03

Visual animation for projection

The cockroach's locomotion is authored specifically for projected light. Because a projector cannot emit black, the animal has to be legible through contrast against the lit surface rather than through rendered detail — a constraint that shaped the animation style directly.

Component 04

Interactivity

The scene responds to the person in the room: the creature's behaviour and the mapping surface both change with the occupant, so the projection is not a loop played at an audience but a system the visitor is part of.

Why projection, not a screen

A screen keeps the creature at a safe distance — it is clearly over there. Projection removes that distance: the animal lands on the cushions, on the wall, and on the person sitting between them. The medium was chosen because it is the only one that lets the digital element occupy the same surface as the body.
