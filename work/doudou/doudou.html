DOU'DOU 兜兜淘梦 🦾 - An experimental robotic art performance
Bottom line

We spend our lives performing to audiences who come and go, and the part of us we hide ends up exiled to our shadow. Dou'Dou stages that shadow's escape: a KUKA robotic arm carries a projected light through the space like a chasing spotlight, threading fragmented dream scenes together until the shadow — not the performer — becomes the protagonist.

1KUKA arm, programmed through Grasshopper to move a projected light

00Executive Summary

What this project is, and why it works

Dou'Dou 兜兜淘梦 is an experimental robotic art performance. Following a fixed-point trajectory, the robotic arm carries a media projection through the space like a chasing light, connecting multiple fragmented dream segments through the interaction of light and shadow with the room. By constructing the “escape plan” of a self-conscious shadow in our sleep, the piece argues that the past we want to escape from is in fact a part of our own heart — and that we are capable of facing our inner self.

  1. The subject is the self we hide, and it is stated as a mechanism rather than a mood. In growing up, everyone performs on the stage of their own life; as audiences come and go, we build a self-protection mechanism — putting on a veil, presenting a false face — and hide the true self somewhere inside. The piece takes that hiding as its starting condition.
  2. The shadow is used as a spectator, which is what makes the metaphor work. Whenever light shines, we acquire a shadow; it follows us and is conventionally read as a subordinate part of the person, a part of the self projected outward. So the shadow is us — we are both performer and audience — and staging its rebellion needs no invented villain.
  3. The research is what turns a feeling into an argument. Three strands converge: the shadow as the spectator of the self; lucid dreaming, where we can be the protagonist and a spectator at the same time; and Marc Levy's Shadow Stealer, whose premise is that a shadow holds thoughts the person does not know about themselves.
  4. The robotic arm is not decoration — it is the staging device. Programming the arm's trajectory lets the projector behave like a theatre's chasing light, moving the audience from one scene to the next. A conventional light cue could not sweep the space in three dimensions, and it is that motion that makes the dream read as continuous rather than as a slideshow.

01Concept

Discipline: conceptual framing

The piece rests on one observation about theatre. Like a chasing light on a stage, a performer is followed from one point to another, bringing the audience into one scene after another. But as performers ourselves we are, more often than not, also the spectators — performing to the best of our ability, immersed in our own world, recording our own memories.

In the dream world, the part of the self that is hidden in reality is no longer repressed. It is as if the shadow — a spectator in reality — has swapped identities with the performer himself. The shadow becomes the protagonist, releasing its true emotions in the dream world, which may make us uncomfortable; that discomfort is precisely the point the piece is working with.

Design intent

The performance deliberately stages the shadow as self-conscious rather than monstrous. Its “escape plan” is not an act of revenge on the performer; it is the hidden self asserting that it belongs to the same person. The ending the piece aims at is not conflict but recognition — we have the ability to face our inner self.

02Research

Discipline: theoretical grounding

The concept is an argument about identity, and it was assembled from three strands — each of which supplies a different part of the case rather than decorating it.

Strand 01

The spectator of self

Whenever a light shines we acquire a shadow, which acts as a spectator of ourselves and always follows — conventionally read as a subordinate part of the person, a part of the self projected outward. This is what establishes that the shadow is us, not an external figure.

Strand 02

The dreaming self

Waking up and realising we were in a dream state describes lucid dreaming — a state in which we can be the protagonist and, on the sidelines, the spectator watching ourselves. It provides the stage on which the shadow can step forward instead of the performer.

Strand 03

The shadow stealer

Marc Levy's Le Voleur d'ombres centres on the idea that a shadow contains thoughts and ideas unknown to the person themselves, and describes what happens when a shadow is able to express itself as an individual — the project's clearest precedent.

03Experiment & Production

Discipline: performance, motion programming & compositing

The production has four moving parts, and they were chosen so that each stage answers one question the concept could not answer on paper: what the shadow looks like, where it appears, how it is lit, and how the light itself should move.

Stage 01

‘Perform’ the shadow

As part of my self-projection, I chose to act out the shadow protagonist myself in the form of my own action performance, with subsequent image processing — rather than casting or animating it.

Stage 02

Scene elements — vignettes

Simple line vignettes — a birdcage, a doorway, a lamp — express the scenes of the dream world, which is conceptualised and abstracted rather than rendered literally.

Stage 03

Compositing

The shadow role was composited into those scenes and given a spotlight-like effect in After Effects, separated by colour and segment the way remembered dream sequences are — which is what lets discrete images read as one continuous dream.

Stage 04

Grasshopper-controlled arm

After planning the duration and interval of the media trajectory, Grasshopper was used to program the KUKA arm, with debugging through repeated tests — the stage that converts the light from a projection into a moving performer.

Why an industrial robot rather than a projector on a track

The concept depends on the audience being moved from scene to scene the way a chasing light moves them in a theatre. A fixed projector washes the whole room at once, which flattens the dream into one image; a linear track can only sweep a line. Programming a six-axis arm lets the projection travel in three dimensions and pause — so the light itself becomes the performer, and the space between scenes can be paced for the audience.
