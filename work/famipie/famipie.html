FamiPie 派家 🎈 — A rapid-response air-film house for post-earthquake PTSD
Bottom line

Four major earthquakes left 22–40% of survivors with PTSD. Emergency shelter keeps people alive, but the standard square, hard, temporary box gives them almost nothing to feel positive about — the shelter itself becomes a second trauma. FamiPie answers with a soft, curved, expandable house: a membrane ceiling that cannot “destroy the roof”, an arc language that erases corners, and pie-slice modules that grow with the family and cluster into a settlement. My part was the 3D production that made it visible — modelling, lighting, rendering and camera animation in Blender, end to end.

Outcome lead

A concept housing system for post-earthquake PTSD, delivered as a Blender-rendered spatial animation.

Featured Outputs

  • Soft over hard. A single-layer membrane ceiling on a triangular frame — if it comes down, it cannot crush anyone.
  • Pie-slice modularity. Four interchangeable modules — AHB wall, window, sliding door with utilities, air film — assemble into a home.
  • Settlement logic. Modules grow with family size, separate zones cleanly, and cluster into a mutual-aid settlement with curved aisles.
  • Full Blender pipeline. Modelling, materials, lighting, camera animation and render, all authored by me.

00Executive Summary

What this project is, and why it works

FamiPie 派家 (Fami from “family”, Pie from the pie-slice plan) is a rapid-response housing concept for people living with post-earthquake PTSD. Rather than a hard temporary shelter, it proposes an air-film house: a soft membrane roof over a light frame, a plan made of pie slices, and a kit of four modules combined according to family size. The project is presented as a rendered spatial animation — and that animation is the part I authored, from the first model to the final camera move in Blender.

  1. It starts from a measured problem, not a mood. Four major earthquakes — Tangshan 1976, Zhangbei 1998, Wenchuan 2008, Haiti 2010 — left 22%, 24%, 40% and 37% of survivors with PTSD respectively. The same research notes that current response measures create very few positive psychological factors for the people living through them.
  2. The shelter itself is part of the trauma. A square, hard, slab-built temporary house reads as a container people are “placed” into, and its hard roof is a constant reminder of what fell. FamiPie removes both cues: the roof is soft, the plan is curved.
  3. The system scales from one family to a settlement. Pie-slice modules expand with family size, separate living zones, and cluster so neighbouring families can help each other through recovery.
  4. The deliverable is a spatial film, not a board. The concept is carried by a 65-second rendered animation that moves through exterior, interior and settlement — the format that lets a viewer feel the space rather than read it.

01Background

Discipline: problem framing & research

The brief comes from the gap between survival and recovery. Emergency shelter is judged by how fast it can be deployed and how many people it can hold; almost nothing in that brief asks how it feels to live in one for months.

Finding 01

PTSD outlasts the earthquake

Across four major earthquakes, 22–40% of survivors developed PTSD — Tangshan 1976 22%, Zhangbei 1998 24%, Wenchuan 2008 40%, Haiti 2010 37%. The need continues long after the tents are gone.

Finding 02

Relief creates few positive factors

Current response measures — emergency shelter, basic medical units, psychological intervention — keep people alive but create very few positive psychological factors, which is what recovery actually needs.

Finding 03

The hard box is a reminder

A square, hard, temporary shelter is a visual echo of the disaster: a hard roof overhead, square corners, and the feeling of being “placed”. The environment works against forgetting.

How might we

How might emergency housing stop reminding survivors of the disaster — and start giving them something to feel positive about?

The problem, measured. Four major earthquakes left 22% (Tangshan 1976), 24% (Zhangbei 1998), 40% (Wenchuan 2008) and 37% (Haiti 2010) of survivors with PTSD — while the current response measures, from emergency shelter to psychological intervention, create very few positive psychological factors for the people living through them.

02Design Response

Discipline: spatial & product concept

The response is built from three moves, each aimed at one of the findings.

Move 01 — Soft roof

A ceiling that cannot destroy the roof

A single-layer flexible membrane replaces the hard ceiling. Even if it collapses, it cannot “destroy the roof” — the fear that the disaster will repeat is removed from the one surface people look at most.

Move 02 — Triangle

A structure that reads as settled

Two hard plates open like a book to form a stable triangle. The structure reads as settled from the inside, even though it is temporary.

Move 03 — Arc

An arc language that erases corners

The plan and the roof use an arc language instead of square corners — deliberately erasing the geometry of the disaster, so the interior feels like a remembered home rather than a container.

The three moves that answer the research. A single-layer flexible membrane replaces the hard ceiling; two plates open like a book into a stable triangle; and an arc language removes the square corners that echo the disaster.

03Modular System

Discipline: modular product design

The house is a kit of four modules, so it can be shipped flat, assembled without heavy machinery, and combined differently for every family.

AHB wall & connection parts Honeycomb aluminium panel frame — the structural kit
Window module Daylight and a view out
Sliding door + water & electricity The service core
Air film The soft roof and skin
15° tilted top Sheds rain and falling objects
Mezzanine blinds Intelligent light control
The kit of four modules — honeycomb aluminium (AHB) wall and connections, window, sliding door with water and electricity, and the air film — with a 15° tilted top to shed rain and falling objects, and mezzanine blinds for light control.

04Settlement Logic

Discipline: settlement & spatial planning

One module is a room; many modules make a settlement. The plan logic is what turns emergency housing from a row of identical boxes into a place where families can recover together.

Expansion

By family size

Pie slices are added or removed so a family gets the space it actually needs, instead of a fixed unit.

Separation

Not “placement”

Different zones are separated within the plan, so a family feels at home rather than “placed”.

Settlement

Mutual aid

Clusters let neighbouring families help each other through the recovery period.

Circulation

Curved aisles

Curved circulation replaces the barracks grid, giving the settlement layers and a horizon instead of a corridor.

The settlement logic. Pie-slice modules expand with family size, separate zones clearly so residents feel at home rather than “placed”, cluster into a mutual-aid settlement, and use curved aisles to give the plan layers instead of a barracks grid.

05Blender Production

Discipline: 3D production — my role

The concept was authored entirely in Blender, as one pipeline: build the kit, assemble the scenes, light them, and animate the camera through them.

Stage 01 — MODEL

A kit, not a monolith

The four modules were built as separate objects, so the same kit could be recombined into a single house, an interior and a settlement without rebuilding anything.

Stage 02 — MATERIAL & LIGHT

Soft, even, readable

Membrane, aluminium and glass were kept in a narrow, soft palette; even lighting keeps the white surfaces reading as fabric rather than plastic.

Stage 03 — CAMERA

Scale through movement

The camera travels from object to interior to settlement, so the same house reads as a product, a room and a neighbourhood in turn.

Stage 04 — RENDER & CUT

Captions instead of narration

Each shot was rendered and cut into a 65-second film with on-screen captions — “Inflatable & soft”, “Modular & Elegant”, “Community Construction” — so the concept reads without a voice-over.

06The Film

The rendered spatial animation

The final deliverable is a 65-second animation that walks through the system: the object, the interior and the settlement.

FamiPie 派家 — the spatial animation. Modelled, lit, rendered and animated in Blender: the house as an object, the interior as a room, and the modules as a settlement.
