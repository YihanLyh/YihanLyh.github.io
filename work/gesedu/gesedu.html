Ges.edu 👌🏻 - Gesture-Assisted Early Childhood Education Platform
Bottom line

Technology spent a century removing the need for hands — and gestures survived anyway. Ges.edu takes that observation seriously: it makes gesture the input of a childhood learning platform, so that children build the interaction habit while they build the knowledge, and are ready for interfaces they will meet decades later.

00Executive Summary

What this project is, and why it works

Ges.edu is a gesture-assisted early childhood education platform that integrates gesture-based interaction into the learning experience in order to strengthen communication and adaptability, for both the child and the institution teaching them.

  1. It argues that gestures are a future skill, not a legacy input. The research starts from a real observation: platforms have moved through physical surfaces, screens, motion sensing and VR without ever removing the gesture. Teaching children gestures early is therefore preparing them for interfaces that do not exist yet.
  2. One question drives the entire design. How might we develop an education platform that uses gestures as the input of interaction, to promote the use of gestures in early education and help develop the habit from the beginning? Every course, screen and evaluation metric in the platform traces back to that sentence.
  3. The teaching form was invented, not assumed. The visual language comes from light painting — both last century's masters and modern VR painting leave a brush trail of light in space. That trail became the mechanism for displaying a gesture: light lines trace the child's hands and highlight the moves they have not completed.
  4. The outcome is a complete system, not a concept. Research framing, curriculum structure, a seven-part information architecture, a four-stage journey, a full UI kit, a mascot, a typeface and a colour system — delivered as something an institution could actually run.

01Design Perspective

Discipline: research & interaction design

The interesting finding underneath the project is that gesture use helps a great deal with daily expression and with learning itself, and that new technology keeps opening new scenarios for it. The design task was therefore not to invent a use for gestures, but to pick the right one and build a habit around it.

Design decision What it is Why it was made
Frame gestures as human-computer, not human-machine Input research moves from physical surface → digital screen → motion sensing → virtual reality. It shows the gesture is the one input that never gets designed away, which is what justifies teaching it rather than a specific device.
Derive the teaching form from light painting Light trails through space, as used by last century's light-painting masters and by modern VR painting. A gesture is invisible and transient. A light trail is the only visual grammar that can record its form and orientation while it happens, so the guide and the gesture share a medium.
Structure the curriculum around gesture fluency Goal, offering, evaluation and supporting layers — including a fluency-of-gesture-use measure. If the habit is the point, it has to be measured. Evaluation against fluency turns gestures from an activity into a learning outcome.
Give the platform a teacher, not just a UI “Teacher Jessy” as a consistent character throughout the product. Early childhood interfaces need a face to trust; a character also carries the gesture notation, so the demonstration and the guidance come from the same source.
Build in competition with oneself Milestone medals and visual progress rather than ranking. Progress visibility motivates children without turning an early education platform into a leaderboard.
Design intent

Gestures are taught in the form of notation — the same way music or dance is taught — and then read back to the child as light. The learning loop is: watch the notation, perform the gesture, see the light trail confirm or highlight what is missing.

02Technical Perspective

Discipline: sensing & adaptive feedback

Technically the platform is built on camera-based hand tracking with AI gesture recognition. The camera must be positioned to recognise the child's hands for the system to focus at all, and everything the platform does — feedback, evaluation, suggestion — is downstream of that recognition.

Component 01

Gesture capture & recognition

Hand tracking reads the child's gestures live; AI recognition classifies them so the system can tell a completed gesture from an attempted one. Camera placement is treated as a setup requirement rather than a hidden assumption.

Component 02

Instruction by highlighting

Sample gestures are drawn as light lines over the child's own hands, and unfinished gestures are highlighted specifically. Correction arrives as a visual gap to close, not as an error message.

Component 03

Recording & self-presentation

Children record themselves performing what they have learned and upload it for parents, which serves the evaluation loop and gives the child a reason to perform the gesture again outside the lesson.

Component 04

Two-source feedback

Final suggestions combine the platform's AI with the institution's teachers. Keeping a human in the loop is what makes the output feel like education rather than automated grading — and it is why the platform is sold to institutions rather than to parents alone.

Why the camera is the whole product

Almost every feature depends on recognition quality, which makes camera positioning a genuine product constraint rather than an installation detail. The interface therefore has to teach the setup — because a child whose hands are not being read correctly is not learning badly, they are not being seen at all.

03Platform & Journey

Structure and staged learning
Research background — gesture as an enduring input across technologies, the human-machine to human-computer shift, the platform definition and its “how might we”, the light-painting teaching-form ideation, and the curriculum's goal / offering / evaluation / supporting layers.
User experience design — the seven-section platform overview, the user journey across before / in / after course and evaluation, the interface screens, and the delivered design system (logo, Alibaba PuHuiTi, palette, mascot and gesture-highlighting samples).

The learning journey

Stage 01

Before course

Children try demo courses and are assessed for ability and interest; parents plan the course with the results.

Stage 02

In course

Gesture-assisted learning is taught as notation, with timely encouragement and highlighted guidance on screen.

Stage 03

After course

The child performs what they learned to their parents and uploads the recording.

Stage 04

Evaluation

Platform AI and the institution's teachers return suggestions; milestones are awarded and progress is visualised.
