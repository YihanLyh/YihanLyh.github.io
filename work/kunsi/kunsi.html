KUN'SI 髡司 🐠 — A digital interactive installation on Han dynasty culture
Bottom line

Cultural heritage is a constant topic of the times, yet the more convenient information becomes, the more selectively young audiences consume it — the obscure parts of a tradition rarely get a second look. KUN'SI answers that with a mechanism rather than a lecture: it lifts one Han dynasty stone-relief motif, the “parade of fish carts”, rebuilds its two elements as a 3D world, and hands the audience the reins. By waving their hands they summon the deity fish, board the cart and travel from a waterfall through ancient forests and towns into the clouds — turning a passive viewer of heritage into its active participant.

00Executive Summary

What this project is, and why it works

KUN'SI 髡司 is an interactive digital installation built on a single Han dynasty stone-relief motif. Standing in front of an L-shaped projection screen, the audience starts the piece with a wave of the hand: the deity fish leap over and connect themselves to the fish cart one by one, and the cart then carries the audience over a waterfall, through ancient towns and finally into a cloud-borne heavenly palace — before the fish turn into a Chinese loong and fly back carrying their blessing. The piece is not a digital replica of a relic; it turns the audience from spectators into the people the story is happening to.

  1. It answers a stated problem, not an imagined one. The research names two barriers facing traditional culture: people selectively accept only what already interests them, and they will not take the initiative to engage with obscure content. That passiveness — not a shortage of material — is the condition the installation is designed against.
  2. The subject is a specific, dated artefact rather than “Chinese culture” in general. Everything derives from the Han dynasty “parade of fish carts” stone relief, a scene traditionally associated with Kunlun, the Queen Mother of the West, the constellations, mythical animals and the deities of the lightning storm.
  3. The transformation work is what makes it legible. The fish is rebuilt as a 3D character from two traits read off the relief — the fat body that symbolised a rich life, and the flapping fin for the fish that shuttles between the surface of the land and the sky — while the cart is rebuilt part by part, so the audience rides a designed object with an anatomy rather than a prop.
  4. The interface is the body. There is no controller: entering the space triggers the fish, and the audience's hand and arm movements summon the fish, drive the cart and steer the view. Immersion here is a control scheme, not a decoration.

01Background Research

Discipline: cultural research & problem framing

Before designing anything, the project set out to understand why traditional culture is talked about so much and understood so little. Three findings shaped the brief — and the third one changed it from an exhibition into an interaction.

Finding 01

Barriers of traditional culture

With more convenient access to information, people are more willing to selectively accept new things — which to some extent hinders the spread of many traditional cultures.

Finding 02

The passiveness of traditional culture

Limited by the information they are already interested in, people will not take the initiative to understand obscure traditional cultural content — holding back its inheritance and development across generations.

Finding 03

Museums lack immersion & empathy

Museums act as a medium for exhibiting traditional culture, but heritage displays, stone restoration, or a caption beside a 10-tab sculpture leave little room for empathy — the visitor reads about the culture without entering it.

How might we

The brief the research produced: how might we use interactive technology to design traditional cultural displays that move the audience from a passive visit to an active experience — and thereby achieve the mobilisation of motivation? That question is what turns a static stone relief into an installation the audience operates.

The story selected from the totems of “the parade of fish carts” scenes, and the definition that follows — a three-level goal (learn about → experience → educate) mapped to offerings and supporting technologies, aimed at an audience split evenly between history, culture and art lovers and parent-child families and student groups.

02Primary Design

Discipline: character & object design

The motif was decomposed into two elements, and each was rebuilt from its own historical logic rather than stylised at will. The names matter: [kun] is the fish, [si] is the cart, and together they give the piece its title.

Element 01 — [kun] 髡

The deity fish, rebuilt as a 3D character

Two traits were read directly off the relief and carried into the model: a fat body — at that time fish were engraved with a full body to symbolise a rich life — and a flapping fin, for the fish that shuttles between the surface of the land and the sky. Its colour is tied to the palette the Han dynasty itself advocated.

Element 02 — [si] 司

The two-wheeled cart, rebuilt part by part

In the Han dynasty the carriage changed from a single wheel to a two-wheeled cart and became an important means of transportation; the fish cart is the imagination extending from it, carrying people's blessing. It was modelled with its real anatomy — 舆 the body, 辋/毂/辐 the wheel, 轭 the yoke, 盖 the canopy, 辀 the shaft, and 軎 the hub cap that gradually became a decoration and status symbol.

Why rebuild rather than illustrate

The piece needs the audience to ride the object, so the objects have to survive being seen from any angle and at close range. A flat illustration collapses the moment the cart turns; a modelled, part-by-part build is what lets the audience stand behind the cart and steer it — the design decision that makes the interaction possible.

The transformation of the motif's elements — the deity fish [kun] modelled into a 3D feature with its fat body and flapping fin, and the cart [si] rebuilt from its Han dynasty anatomy as the object the audience takes control of.

03Ideation & Experiment

Discipline: interaction & spatial design

The installation has to make a first-time visitor act within seconds, without reading instructions. Two decisions do that work — how the space is built, and how the interaction starts.

Space

An L-shaped screen in a blacked-out room

A black, light-absorbing material lines the space to remove reflection and form an immersive environment. The L-shaped screen is used deliberately to create a perspective effect within a limited footprint: one projector drives the front wall, a second drives the ground screen, and a marked viewing spot defines the interaction zone.

Sensing

Kinect v2 reads the whole body

A Kinect v2 movement sensor detects the whole-body posture and movement of the audience, so the interaction needs no handheld device — the audience's own position and gestures are the input.

Interaction

How the interaction starts

1 — When the audience enters the space the device recognises them and a real fish leaps over. 2 — Getting to the screen summons the fish, and the cart, one by one. 3 — The fish cart then takes the audience toward the temple, passing over the sea and carrying the blessing of traditional myth.

Test

Construction & primary testing

A primary demo tested whether the cart could fly and be controlled by the gesture of a hand. Students controlled the cart by hand gesture while the interaction method was demonstrated, and the layout, program and gesture recognition were refined from what the test showed.

Why an L-shaped screen and a gesture, not a flat screen and a mouse

The story is a journey forward, and a flat screen would keep it at arm's length as a picture. The L-shape wraps the audience inside the projected world so the scene reads as a space rather than an image, and gesture removes the last piece of hardware between the audience and the story — the audience's own body becomes the reins of the cart.

04Develop

Discipline: production pipeline

Production ran as four tracks that fed one another — art, concept, programming and test — under a single constraint: the media had to be authored for a shaped screen rather than a rectangle.

Track 01 — ART

Modeling, texture & animation

Modelling and texture painting, animation with Unity Timeline, and scene optimisation. The format of presentation — the L-shaped projection screen — was settled here, including the mirror value needed to build a 3D effect and create the feeling of walking.

Track 02 — CONCEPT

The interaction flow

The elements and scenes were planned and designed here: 1 get to the viewing spot, 2 summon the fish, 3 control the fish's movement, 4 enjoy the multi-sensory experience — a flow written to be understood the moment the audience steps in.

Track 03 — PROGRAMMING

Real-time system & gesture input

Coding and debugging with parameter optimisation: 3D technology for visualisation and interaction in Unity, the image broadcast over NDI, with Kinect gesture input and animation driving the scenes in real time.

Track 04 — TEST → CURATION

Feedback, construction & curation

Feedback and design optimisations looped back into the previous tracks, followed by construction and curation — the stage that turns a working prototype into an exhibition a stranger can walk into and operate.

05Final Demo

Wide-angle main scenes on the L-shaped projection screen

The finished piece runs as one continuous journey. It opens with interactive instructions, and the fish carts move forward over the waterfall and through the ancient towns; at the end it folds out and returns to the initial screen after the audience leaves.

  1. Scene 1 — Waterfalls and hills. The journey begins where the relief's water does.
  2. Scene 2 — Ancient forest. The cart moves forward through dense, untamed terrain.
  3. Scene 3 — Ancient towns. The cart passes a built, inhabited world.
  4. Scene 4 — Heavenly palace on clouds. The journey lifts out of the land and into the sky, the destination of the myth.
  5. Scene 5 — Turn into loong & bring blessings. The fish fly away, turn into a Chinese loong and fly back — the blessing the traditional myth carries.
The storyline and its interactive method. Beginning: the audience waves their hands back and forth, and four fish leap over and connect to the cart one by one. Travel through scenes: moving their arms left and right, the audience steers the cart forward and gains a more comprehensive view. Ending: the fish rush into the sky, fly away, turn into a Chinese loong and fly back — symbolising the blessing in traditional myth.

06Delivery

Exhibition & on-site findings

The piece was delivered as a complete exhibition stand rather than a lone screen, so that the work could be explained, remembered and re-entered by visitors who had never heard of the motif.

Exhibit

The stand

Exhibit wall · project introduction · promotional video · model ornament · posters · entrance mat · cushion — arranged so a visitor meets the story before they meet the screen.

Audience perspective

The two documented interactions

“Summon the fish” and “Control the cart” were captured from the audience's own point of view — evidence that the interaction read as intended without a demonstrator.

On site ++++ — what the exhibition showed

Through this interactive experience, young people gain a better understanding of the traditional cultural stories — and are therefore more likely to inherit and develop them. During the exhibition the audience actively participated in the interaction, and the piece received the recognition of Chinese culture experts along with suggestions for further development.

The delivered exhibition space — exhibit wall, project introduction, promotional video, model ornament, posters, entrance mat and cushion — alongside audience-perspective renders of the two interactions: summoning the fish and controlling the cart.
